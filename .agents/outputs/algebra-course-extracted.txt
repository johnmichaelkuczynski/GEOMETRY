1.1 Real numbers and operations
The number system algebra is built on.
Real numbers and operations
Algebra is arithmetic with letters standing in for numbers, so it begins with the
numbers themselves. The real numbers include the natural numbers 1, 2, 3, ..., the
integers (adding 0 and the negatives), the rationals (every fraction p/q with q ≠ 0),
and the irrationals such as √2 and π, whose decimals never end or repeat.
Signed numbers
The absolute value |a| is the distance from a to 0 on the number line, so |−7| = |7| =
7. Adding a negative is subtracting, and subtracting a negative is adding. For products
and quotients:
(−)(−) = (+), (−)(+) = (−).
Thus (−3)(−4) = 12 and −20 / 4 = −5. Division by zero is undefined: no number times 0
gives 5.
Opposites and reciprocals
Every number a has an opposite −a with a + (−a) = 0, and every nonzero number has
a reciprocal 1/a with a · (1/a) = 1. Subtraction is adding the opposite; division is
multiplying by the reciprocal. These two facts are the engine of equation solving in
Lecture 1.4.
A real example
In 628 CE the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta wrote the Brahmasphutasiddhanta,
the first text to treat zero as a number and to state rules for negatives. He called
positive numbers fortunes and negative numbers debts: "a debt minus zero is a debt,"
"the product of two debts is a fortune." European mathematicians were still calling
negative solutions "absurd" a thousand years later.

1.2 Order of operations and algebraic expressions
Reading an expression the only way it can be read.
Order of operations and algebraic expressions
An algebraic expression combines numbers, variables, and operations: 3x + 5, 2(a −
b)2. To evaluate one, substitute values for the variables and compute.
The order of operations
A single expression must have a single value, so a convention fixes the order:
•
Grouping symbols first — parentheses, brackets, fraction bars, radicals;
•
then exponents;
•
then multiplication and division, left to right;
•
then addition and subtraction, left to right.
3 + 4 · 22 = 3 + 4 · 4 = 19.
Translating words into symbols
"Five less than twice a number" is 2n − 5, not 5 − 2n. "The square of the sum of x and
y" is (x + y)2; "the sum of the squares" is x2 + y2. Most word-problem errors are
translation errors, made before any algebra begins.
A real example
In 2019 the expression 8 ÷ 2(2 + 2) went viral, splitting millions of people between 16
and 1. Strict left-to-right gives 16; treating 2(2 + 2) as a single unit gives 1, and some
older textbooks and calculators did exactly that. The dispute is a notation failure, not
an arithmetic one — which is why programming languages require an explicit
multiplication sign and why careful writers use parentheses.
1.3 Properties and simplifying expressions
The rules that let you rewrite an expression without changing it.
Properties and simplifying expressions
Two expressions are equivalent if they give the same value for every value of the
variables. A few properties generate every legal rewrite.
The basic properties
•
Commutative: a + b = b + a and ab = ba;
•
Associative: (a + b) + c = a + (b + c) and (ab)c = a(bc);
•
Identity: a + 0 = a and a · 1 = a;
•
Distributive: multiplication spreads over addition.
a(b + c) = ab + ac.

Combining like terms
Like terms have the same variables raised to the same powers: 4x and −7x are like, 4x
and 4x2 are not. The distributive property run backwards combines them: 4x − 7x = (4
− 7)x = −3x. So 3(2x − 5) − (x − 4) = 6x − 15 − x + 4 = 5x − 11. A minus sign in front
of parentheses distributes −1 to every term inside.
A real example
Around 300 BCE Euclid's Elements, Book II, Proposition 1, proved the distributive law
as geometry: a rectangle of height a cut into strips of widths b, c, and d has area equal
to the sum of the strips' areas, a(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ad. Greek mathematicians had
no algebraic notation, so every identity in this lecture was, for them, a statement about
areas.
1.4 Solving one- and two-step equations
Undoing operations while keeping the balance.
Solving one- and two-step equations
An equation asserts that two expressions are equal. A solution is a value of the
variable that makes the assertion true. Solving means producing simpler equivalent
equations until the variable stands alone.
The properties of equality
Whatever you do to one side, do to the other: add, subtract, multiply, or divide (by a
nonzero number) on both sides and the solutions are unchanged.
a = b ⇒ a + c = b + c and ac = bc.
Two-step equations
Undo operations in reverse order: addition and subtraction first, then multiplication
and division. For 3x − 7 = 11, add 7 to get 3x = 18, then divide by 3 to get x = 6. Check
by substituting: 3(6) − 7 = 11. Checking catches arithmetic errors at a cost of seconds.
A real example
The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied by the Egyptian scribe Ahmes around 1550
BCE, contains "aha" problems — the first recorded linear equations. Problem 24 reads:
a quantity and its seventh, added together, become 19. In modern symbols x + x/7 =
19, so 8x/7 = 19 and x = 16 5/8. Ahmes reached the same answer by guessing 7, seeing
it gave 8, and scaling up — the method of false position.

1.5 Multi-step equations
Variables on both sides, grouping, and fractions.
Multi-step equations
Harder equations need several moves, but the plan never changes: simplify each side,
collect the variable on one side, then isolate it.
The procedure
•
Clear fractions by multiplying both sides by the least common denominator.
•
Distribute and combine like terms on each side.
•
Add or subtract to move variable terms to one side and constants to the other.
•
Divide by the coefficient, then check.
5x + 3 = 2x − 9 ⇒ 3x = −12 ⇒ x = −4.
No solution and infinitely many
Sometimes the variable cancels. If a false statement remains (2x + 1 = 2x + 5 gives 1 =
5), there is no solution. If a true statement remains (2(x + 3) = 2x + 6 gives 6 = 6),
the equation is an identity and every real number is a solution.
A real example
A puzzle from the Greek Anthology, around 500 CE, gives the life of the mathematician
Diophantus: boyhood lasted 1/6 of his life, youth 1/12 more, then 1/7 more before
marriage; a son was born 5 years later and lived half as long as his father, who died 4
years after the son. So x = x/6 + x/12 + x/7 + 5 + x/2 + 4. Multiplying by 84 clears the
fractions: 84x = 75x + 756, so x = 84.
1.6 Literal equations and formulas
Solving for one letter in terms of the others.
Literal equations and formulas
A literal equation has several variables. Solving it for one of them uses exactly the
moves of Lecture 1.5, treating the other letters as if they were numbers.
Rearranging formulas
Distance equals rate times time, d = rt, solved for t gives t = d/r. The area of a
trapezoid A = ½h(b1 + b2) solved for h gives h = 2A/(b1 + b2). Temperature:
F = (9/5)C + 32 ⇒ C = (5/9)(F − 32).
Units as a check
Units obey algebra too. If d is in miles and r in miles per hour, then d/r has units miles
÷ (miles/hour) = hours. A rearranged formula whose units come out wrong has been
rearranged wrongly.

A real example
In 1983 Air Canada Flight 143, a new Boeing 767, ran out of fuel at 41,000 feet over
Manitoba. The crew needed 22,300 kilograms of fuel and computed the volume to load
from volume = mass ÷ density, but used 1.77 — the density in pounds per liter, not
kilograms per liter. The plane took off with less than half the fuel required. The captain
glided it to a safe landing at a former air base in Gimli.
1.7 Ratios, proportions, and percent
Comparing quantities by division.
Ratios, proportions, and percent
A ratio compares two quantities by division, a : b or a/b. A rate is a ratio of quantities
in different units, such as 60 miles per hour. A proportion states that two ratios are
equal.
Solving proportions
Multiplying both sides of a/b = c/d by bd gives the cross-product rule:
a/b = c/d ⇒ ad = bc.
If 3 pounds cost $7.50, then 3/7.50 = 8/x gives 3x = 60 and x = $20 for 8 pounds.
Percent
Percent means per hundred: 35% = 0.35. The percent equation is part = percent ×
whole. Percent change is (new − old)/old. A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease
is not a return to start: 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, a net loss of 4%.
A real example
Around 240 BCE Eratosthenes learned that at noon on the summer solstice the sun
shone straight down a well at Syene, while at Alexandria, about 5,000 stadia north, it
cast a shadow at an angle of about 7.2°. Since 7.2° is 1/50 of a full circle, the
proportion 7.2/360 = 5,000/C gives Earth's circumference as 250,000 stadia — close to
the true value, from one shadow and one ratio.

2.1 Linear inequalities
Solving when the answer is a range, not a number.
Linear inequalities
An inequality compares expressions with <, >, ≤, or ≥. Its solution is usually an
infinite set of numbers, drawn as a ray on the number line — an open circle for < or >,
a closed circle for ≤ or ≥.
The one new rule
Inequalities are solved like equations, with one exception: multiplying or dividing both
sides by a negative number reverses the direction of the inequality.
−3x > 12 ⇒ x < −4.
The reason: −3 < 2, but multiplying by −1 gives 3 > −2. Negation reflects the number
line and reverses order.
Interval notation
The set x < −4 is written (−∞, −4); the set x ≥ 2 is [2, ∞). Parentheses exclude an
endpoint and brackets include it. Word problems use "at least" for ≥ and "at most" for
≤.
A real example
The symbols < and > first appeared in print in 1631, in Artis Analyticae Praxis, a book
by the English mathematician Thomas Harriot published ten years after his death.
Harriot, who had sailed to Roanoke as Walter Raleigh's scientific adviser, also drew the
moon through a telescope months before Galileo. The ≤ and ≥ signs came a century
later, from the French mathematician Pierre Bouguer.
2.2 Compound and absolute-value statements
Two conditions at once, and distance from a target.
Compound and absolute-value statements
A compound inequality joins two inequalities. "And" requires both, giving an overlap
such as −2 < x ≤ 5. "Or" requires at least one, giving two pieces such as x < −1 or x >
3.

Algebra 1 — A Four-Week Course · 7
Absolute-value equations
Since |x| is distance from 0, |x − c| is distance from c. An equation |A| = k with k > 0
splits into two cases, A = k or A = −k. So |2x − 1| = 7 gives x = 4 or x = −3. If k < 0
there is no solution.
Absolute-value inequalities
Distance less than k is an "and"; distance greater than k is an "or":
|A| < k ⇔ −k < A < k, |A| > k ⇔ A < −k or A > k.
Thus |x − 10| ≤ 3 means 7 ≤ x ≤ 13: every number within 3 of 10.
A real example
Manufacturing runs on absolute-value inequalities. A shaft specified as 25.00 ± 0.05
millimeters must satisfy |d − 25.00| ≤ 0.05. Such tolerances make interchangeable
parts possible — an idea the French gunsmith Honoré Blanc demonstrated in 1785 by
assembling musket locks from randomly chosen parts, a show that so impressed
Thomas Jefferson that he urged it on the United States.
2.3 Relations and functions
Rules that assign each input exactly one output.
Relations and functions
A relation is any set of input–output pairs. A function is a relation in which each input
has exactly one output. The set of inputs is the domain; the set of outputs is the range.
Function notation
Writing f(x) names the function f and its input x. If f(x) = 2x2 − 3, then
f(4) = 2(16) − 3 = 29.
The notation f(x) does not mean f times x. It means "the output of f when the input is x."
Representations and the vertical line test
A function can be given by a table, an equation, a graph, or words. A graph represents
a function exactly when every vertical line crosses it at most once; the circle x2 + y2 =
25 fails, since x = 3 meets it at y = 4 and y = −4. The independent variable is the
input; the dependent variable is the output.
A real example
Gottfried Leibniz introduced the word "function" in the 1690s, and in 1734 Leonhard
Euler introduced the notation f(x). Euler's notation spread through his textbooks, which
taught Europe mathematics for a century. The modern definition — any rule assigning
one output to each input, formula or not — was settled by Peter Gustav Lejeune
Dirichlet in 1837.

2.4 Slope and rate of change
How steep a line is, as a number.
Slope and rate of change
The slope of a line measures its steepness: the vertical change per unit of horizontal
change. Between two points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2):
The slope formula
m = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1).
Rise over run. From (1, 2) to (4, 11) the slope is 9/3 = 3: y rises 3 for every 1 that x
increases.
Reading slope
Positive slope rises left to right; negative slope falls; zero slope is horizontal; a vertical
line has undefined slope, since its run is zero. In applications slope is a rate of
change with units — dollars per hour, miles per gallon, degrees per minute. A function
has constant rate of change exactly when its graph is a line.
A real example
Accessibility standards issued under the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 cap
wheelchair ramps at a slope of 1/12 — one inch of rise for every twelve inches of run. A
ramp climbing to a 30-inch porch must therefore run at least 30 × 12 = 360 inches, or
30 feet. Road signs state the same idea as a percent: a 6% grade rises 6 feet per 100
feet of road.
2.5 Slope-intercept and point-slope forms
Writing the equation of a line.
Slope-intercept and point-slope forms
Every nonvertical line is the graph of a linear function. Two forms of its equation cover
nearly every use.
Slope-intercept form
y = mx + b.
Here m is the slope and b is the y-intercept, where the line crosses the y-axis. To
graph y = −⅔x + 4, plot (0, 4), then move down 2 and right 3. In a model, b is the
starting value and m the rate: a taxi charging $3 plus $2.50 per mile costs y = 2.5x + 3.
Point-slope form
Given a slope m and any point (x1, y1) on the line, y − y1 = m(x − x1). Given two points,
find m with the slope formula, then use either point. This is the fastest route from data
to an equation.

A real example
Water freezes at 0°C and 32°F and boils at 100°C and 212°F. These two points fix a
line: its slope is (212 − 32)/(100 − 0) = 1.8, and point-slope form through (0, 32) gives
F = 1.8C + 32. Every thermometer conversion is this line. Setting F = C shows the two
scales agree at exactly −40°.
2.6 Standard form, parallel and perpendicular lines
Other forms, and how lines relate.
Standard form, parallel and perpendicular lines
A line can also be written in standard form Ax + By = C. It is the natural form for
intercepts: set y = 0 to find the x-intercept and x = 0 to find the y-intercept. For 3x +
4y = 12 these are (4, 0) and (0, 3).
Special lines
A horizontal line has equation y = k and slope 0. A vertical line has equation x = h
and undefined slope; it is not a function.
Parallel and perpendicular
Distinct nonvertical lines are parallel exactly when their slopes are equal. They are
perpendicular exactly when their slopes are negative reciprocals:
m1 = m2 (parallel), m1 · m2 = −1 (perpendicular).
The perpendicular to y = 2x + 1 through (4, 0) has slope −½, so y = −½(x − 4).
A real example
In 1637 René Descartes published La Géométrie as an appendix to his Discourse on
Method. It joined algebra to geometry by labeling points with number pairs, so that
curves become equations and geometric questions become algebra problems. Whether
two lines are perpendicular — once a matter of compass and straightedge — became a
one-line check on their slopes.
2.7 Linear models and scatter plots
Fitting a line to real, messy data.
Linear models and scatter plots
Real data never fall exactly on a line. A scatter plot shows paired data as points; if
they cluster around a line, a linear model summarizes the trend.
Correlation and the line of best fit
The correlation coefficient r, between −1 and 1, measures how closely the points
hug a line: near ±1 is strong, near 0 is weak. The line of best fit, or least-squares line,
minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances from the points to the line:

Using and misusing a model
Predicting inside the range of the data is interpolation; predicting beyond it is
extrapolation, which is riskier. The slope interprets as a rate and the intercept as a
starting value — when either makes sense. And correlation is not causation: ice cream
sales and drownings rise together because both follow summer.
A real example
In 1929 Edwin Hubble plotted the speeds of 24 galaxies against their distances. The
points scattered widely, but a line through them showed speed proportional to
distance, v = H0d. The slope H0 — the Hubble constant — measures how fast the
universe is expanding, and its reciprocal estimates the age of the universe. Hubble's
slope was about 500; the modern value is about 70.

3.1 Systems of equations by graphing
Where two lines meet.
Systems of equations by graphing
A system of linear equations is two or more equations considered together. A
solution satisfies every equation at once; for two lines, it is their point of intersection.
Three possibilities
•
Lines with different slopes meet once: one solution.
•
Parallel lines never meet: no solution (inconsistent).
•
Identical lines meet everywhere: infinitely many solutions (dependent).
y = 2x − 1, y = −x + 5 ⇒ (2, 3).
Strengths and limits of graphing
Graphing shows at a glance which case you are in, but reading a point such as (1.37,
2.84) off a sketch is imprecise. Use graphs to see and algebra (Lecture 3.2) to compute.
Break-even problems are the classic application: cost and revenue lines cross where
profit is zero.
A real example
Before satellite navigation, a ship's navigator fixed position by taking compass bearings
on two known landmarks and drawing each as a line on the chart. The ship lay on both
lines, so it lay at their intersection — a "fix." A third bearing served as a check: if the
three lines formed a large triangle rather than meeting at a point, a bearing was
wrong.
3.2 Substitution and elimination
Solving systems exactly.
Substitution and elimination
Two algebraic methods solve any system exactly by reducing two equations in two
unknowns to one equation in one unknown.

Algebra 1 — A Four-Week Course · 12
Substitution
Solve one equation for one variable and substitute into the other. From y = 3x and x +
y = 12: x + 3x = 12, so x = 3 and y = 9. Best when a variable already has coefficient 1.
Elimination
Add or subtract the equations, after multiplying one or both by constants, so that one
variable cancels:
2x + 3y = 12, 4x − 3y = 6 ⇒ 6x = 18 ⇒ x = 3, y = 2.
If both variables cancel, a false result such as 0 = 7 means no solution; 0 = 0 means
infinitely many. Mixture, investment, and speed problems all reduce to systems solved
this way.
A real example
The Chinese text Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, compiled around the first
century CE, devotes its eighth chapter, Fangcheng, to systems of linear equations.
Coefficients were laid out as columns of counting rods on a board, and one column was
repeatedly multiplied and subtracted from another until unknowns were eliminated —
the method Europe would credit to Carl Friedrich Gauss some 1,700 years later.
3.3 Systems of linear inequalities
Regions of the plane, and the best point in them.
Systems of linear inequalities
The graph of a linear inequality such as y > 2x − 3 is a half-plane: the boundary line,
dashed for < or > and solid for ≤ or ≥, plus the shaded side that satisfies the
inequality.
Graphing a system
Test a point not on the boundary — (0, 0) when possible — to choose the side to shade.
The solution of a system is the overlap of all the half-planes, the feasible region.
Linear programming
To maximize or minimize a linear quantity such as profit P = ax + by over a feasible
region bounded by lines, check only the corners:
the optimum of P = ax + by occurs at a vertex.
A bakery limited by oven hours and flour finds its most profitable mix of bread and cake
by evaluating profit at each corner of the region its constraints enclose.

A real example
In 1945 the economist George Stigler asked for the cheapest diet meeting nine
nutritional requirements from 77 foods — a system of linear inequalities. With no
general method he found a near-optimal answer of $39.93 a year at 1939 prices. In
1947 George Dantzig invented the simplex method, which walks from corner to corner
of the feasible region; applied to Stigler's problem, it found the true minimum, $39.69.
3.4 Laws of exponents
Shorthand for repeated multiplication, and its rules.
Laws of exponents
For a positive integer n, an means a multiplied by itself n times; a is the base and n the
exponent. Counting factors proves the laws.
The laws
aman = am+n, am/an = am−n, (am)n = amn.
Also (ab)n = anbn and (a/b)n = an/bn. But (a + b)n is not an + bn: exponents distribute
over products, not sums.
Zero and negative exponents
Keeping the quotient law true forces the definitions a0 = 1 and a−n = 1/an for a ≠ 0. So
2−3 = 1/8 and 50 = 1. A negative exponent means reciprocal, never a negative number.
A real example
Around 250 BCE Archimedes wrote The Sand Reckoner to show that the number of
grains of sand needed to fill the universe, though enormous, is finite. Greek numerals
stopped at a myriad, 10,000, so he built a system of orders of magnitude and proved
that multiplying numbers adds their orders — in modern terms aman = am+n. His
estimate came to about 1063 grains.
3.5 Scientific notation and exponential functions
Very large, very small, and very fast.
Scientific notation and exponential functions
Scientific notation writes a number as a × 10n with 1 ≤ a < 10: the speed of light is
3.0 × 108 m/s, a hydrogen atom about 1 × 10−10 m wide. The laws of exponents
multiply such numbers: (2 × 103)(4 × 105) = 8 × 108.
Exponential functions
In a linear function a fixed amount is added each step; in an exponential function a
fixed factor multiplies:
y = a · bx, b > 0, b ≠ 1.

Here a is the initial value. If b > 1 the function shows growth; if 0 < b < 1, decay.
Growth and decay rates
A quantity growing r percent per period is y = a(1 + r)t; decaying, y = a(1 − r)t. $1,000
at 5% compound interest grows to 1,000(1.05)10 ≈ $1,629 in ten years. Exponential
growth always eventually overtakes linear growth, however large the linear slope.
A real example
In 1965 Gordon Moore, later a co-founder of Intel, observed that the number of
transistors on a chip had doubled every year, and predicted it would continue; in 1975
he revised the period to two years. Doubling every two years is y = a · 2t/2. Moore's law
held for about five decades, carrying chips from dozens of transistors to tens of billions.
3.6 Polynomials and their operations
Adding, subtracting, and multiplying expressions.
Polynomials and their operations
A polynomial is a sum of terms of the form axn with whole-number exponents, such as
4x3 − 2x + 7. Its degree is the highest exponent; the term with that exponent is the
leading term. Polynomials are named by degree (linear, quadratic, cubic) and by
number of terms (monomial, binomial, trinomial).
Adding and subtracting
Combine like terms. To subtract, distribute the minus sign to every term of the second
polynomial: (5x2 + 3x − 1) − (2x2 − x + 4) = 3x2 + 4x − 5.
Multiplying
Multiply every term of the first by every term of the second — the distributive property
repeated. For two binomials this is FOIL (first, outer, inner, last):
(x + 3)(x − 5) = x2 − 5x + 3x − 15 = x2 − 2x − 15.
The degree of a product is the sum of the degrees.
A real example
In 1591 the French lawyer François Viète published In Artem Analyticem Isagoge, the
first work to use letters systematically for both unknowns (vowels) and known
quantities (consonants). Before Viète every problem was stated with specific numbers;
afterward a single expression could describe infinitely many problems at once, and a
polynomial became an object that could itself be manipulated.

3.7 Special products
Patterns worth memorizing.
Special products
Three products occur so often that recognizing them saves time in both directions —
multiplying now, factoring in Week 4.
Squares of binomials
(a + b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2, (a − b)2 = a2 − 2ab + b2.
The middle term 2ab is exactly what is forgotten when students write (a + b)2 = a2 +
b2. Mental arithmetic: 512 = 2,500 + 100 + 1 = 2,601.
Sum times difference
(a + b)(a − b) = a2 − b2: the middle terms cancel. So 49 × 51 = 502 − 1 = 2,499.
Higher powers of a + b follow Pascal's triangle, each row formed by adding adjacent
entries of the row above: (a + b)3 = a3 + 3a2b + 3ab2 + b3.
A real example
The triangle of binomial coefficients is named for Blaise Pascal, whose treatise on it
was written in 1654. But the Chinese mathematician Yang Hui printed it in 1261,
crediting Jia Xian, who used it around 1050; in China it is still called Yang Hui's
triangle. The Persian poet and mathematician Omar Khayyam also knew the binomial
expansions in the eleventh century.

4.1 Factoring: GCF and grouping
Running multiplication in reverse.
Factoring: GCF and grouping
To factor a polynomial is to write it as a product. Factoring undoes multiplication and
is the key to solving polynomial equations.
The greatest common factor
Always begin by factoring out the greatest common factor of all terms — the largest
number and highest power of each variable dividing every term:
6x3 + 9x2 − 15x = 3x(2x2 + 3x − 5).
Factoring by grouping
With four terms, group them in pairs, factor each pair, and look for a common binomial
factor: x3 + 2x2 + 3x + 6 = x2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) = (x2 + 3)(x + 2). Check any
factorization by multiplying back.
A real example
Around 300 BCE Euclid's Elements, Book VII, gave an algorithm for the greatest
common divisor of two numbers: repeatedly replace the larger by the remainder when
it is divided by the smaller, until the remainder is zero. For 252 and 105: 252 = 2 · 105
+ 42, 105 = 2 · 42 + 21, 42 = 2 · 21, so the GCD is 21. The Euclidean algorithm is still
used billions of times a day inside encryption software.
4.2 Factoring trinomials
Finding the two binomials that multiply to a quadratic.
Factoring trinomials
A quadratic trinomial ax2 + bx + c often factors into two binomials. Factoring means
reversing FOIL.
When a = 1
Find two numbers whose product is c and whose sum is b:
x2 + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q), pq = c, p + q = b.

For x2 − x − 12 you need product −12 and sum −1: the pair −4 and 3, so (x − 4)(x + 3).
A positive c means the two numbers share b's sign; a negative c means they have

When a ≠ 1, and special patterns
Find two numbers with product ac and sum b, split the middle term, and factor by
grouping: 6x2 + 11x + 4 has ac = 24, split as 3 and 8, giving (3x + 4)(2x + 1).
Recognize the special products of Lecture 3.7 in reverse: a2 − b2 = (a + b)(a − b), and
a2 ± 2ab + b2 = (a ± b)2. A polynomial with no factorization over the integers is prime.
A real example
Factoring is easy to check and hard to do: multiplying two large primes takes a
computer a moment, but recovering them from their product can take longer than the
age of the universe. In 1977 Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman built the
RSA encryption system on exactly this asymmetry. Every secure web connection rests
on the difficulty of factoring.
4.3 Solving quadratics by factoring
The zero-product property.
Solving quadratics by factoring
A quadratic equation has the form ax2 + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0. Its solutions are also
called roots or zeros.
The zero-product property
If a product is zero, at least one factor is zero:
ab = 0 ⇒ a = 0 or b = 0.
So to solve a quadratic, move everything to one side, factor, and set each factor equal
to zero. From x2 − x − 12 = 0: (x − 4)(x + 3) = 0, so x = 4 or x = −3.
The classic error
The property works only for zero. From (x − 2)(x − 3) = 6 you may not conclude x − 2
= 6: expand to x2 − 5x = 0, then factor as x(x − 5) = 0, so x = 0 or x = 5. Dividing both
sides by x loses the solution x = 0 — factor instead.
A real example
A Babylonian clay tablet from about 1800 BCE, now in the British Museum as BM
13901, poses the problem: "I added the area and the side of my square: 3/4." In modern
terms x2 + x = 3/4, which factors as (x − 1/2)(x + 3/2) = 0. The scribe's recipe produces
the answer 1/2. Negative lengths made no sense, so the second root, −3/2, was never
considered.

4.4 Graphing quadratic functions
Parabolas: vertex, axis, and intercepts.
Graphing quadratic functions
The graph of f(x) = ax2 + bx + c is a parabola. It opens upward if a > 0 and downward
if a < 0; a larger |a| makes it narrower.
Vertex and axis of symmetry
The parabola is symmetric about a vertical line through its vertex, the lowest or
highest point:
x = −b / (2a).
For f(x) = x2 − 4x + 1 the axis is x = 2 and the vertex is (2, f(2)) = (2, −3), the
minimum.
Vertex form and intercepts
Vertex form f(x) = a(x − h)2 + k shows the vertex (h, k) directly, as a shift of y = x2.
The y-intercept is c; the x-intercepts are the roots of the equation f(x) = 0. A parabola
may cross the x-axis twice, touch it once at the vertex, or miss it entirely — matching
two, one, or no real solutions.
A real example
In 1638 Galileo Galilei published Two New Sciences, proving that a projectile's path is
a parabola: horizontal motion at constant speed combined with vertical fall that grows
with the square of time. A ball thrown upward at 48 feet per second from 6 feet has
height h = −16t2 + 48t + 6. The vertex, at t = 48/32 = 1.5 seconds, gives the peak
height, 42 feet.
4.5 Completing the square and the quadratic formula
Solving every quadratic.
Completing the square and the quadratic formula
Many quadratics do not factor over the integers. Two methods solve them all.
Completing the square
Since (x + k)2 = x2 + 2kx + k2, add the square of half the x-coefficient to both sides.
For x2 + 6x = 7: add 9 to get (x + 3)2 = 16, so x + 3 = ±4 and x = 1 or x = −7. The
same move converts standard form to vertex form.
The quadratic formula
Completing the square on ax2 + bx + c = 0 in general gives
x = [−b ± √(b2 − 4ac)] / (2a).
The discriminant b2 − 4ac decides the number of real solutions: positive, two; zero,
one; negative, none, since no real number has a negative square.

A real example
Around 820 CE Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, working at the House of Wisdom in
Baghdad, solved x2 + 10x = 39 by literally completing a square: a square of side x with
two rectangles 5 by x attached, then a missing corner of area 25 filled in to make a
larger square of area 64 and side 8, so x = 3. The title of his book, al-jabr, gave us the
word algebra, and his name gave us algorithm.
4.6 Radicals and the Pythagorean theorem
Square roots and right triangles.
Radicals and the Pythagorean theorem
The principal square root √a of a ≥ 0 is the nonnegative number whose square is a.
Numbers such as 49 are perfect squares; the square root of any other positive integer
is irrational.
Simplifying radicals
√(ab) = √a · √b, √(a/b) = √a / √b.
Factor out perfect squares: √72 = √36 · √2 = 6√2. Add only like radicals, 3√5 + 4√5 =
7√5. Rationalize a denominator by multiplying top and bottom by the radical: 1/√2 =
√2/2. Note √(a + b) ≠ √a + √b.
The Pythagorean theorem
In a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c, a2 + b2 = c2. It gives the
distance formula d = √[(x2 − x1)2 + (y2 − y1)2]. A 3-4-5 triangle is right; carpenters
check a square corner by measuring 3 feet, 4 feet, and a 5-foot diagonal.
A real example
A Babylonian tablet from about 1800 BCE, YBC 7289 at Yale, shows a square with its
diagonals and the number 1;24,51,10 in base 60 — about 1.41421296. The true value of
√2 is 1.41421356..., so the scribe was correct to six decimal places. The tablet shows
that the diagonal of a unit square is √2, a Pythagorean fact known more than a
thousand years before Pythagoras.
4.7 Rational expressions
Algebraic fractions.
Rational expressions
A rational expression is a quotient of polynomials, such as (x + 1)/(x2 − 4). It is
undefined wherever its denominator is zero — here at x = 2 and x = −2 — and those
values are excluded from its domain.

Algebra 1 — A Four-Week Course · 20
Simplifying and operating
Factor numerator and denominator, then cancel common factors — never common
terms:
(x2 − 9)/(x2 + 3x) = (x + 3)(x − 3) / [x(x + 3)] = (x − 3)/x.
Multiply and divide as with numerical fractions. To add or subtract, rewrite over a
common denominator.
Rational equations and inverse variation
Multiply both sides by the least common denominator to clear fractions, solve, and then
discard any extraneous solution that makes an original denominator zero. When y =
k/x, y varies inversely with x: doubling one halves the other. Work problems combine
rates: if one pipe fills a tank in 3 hours and another in 6, together they fill 1/3 + 1/6 =
1/2 tank per hour, so 2 hours.
A real example
Every camera focuses by a rational equation. A lens of focal length f images an object
at distance do at distance di behind it, where 1/f = 1/do + 1/di. For a 50 mm lens and a
subject 2,000 mm away, 1/di = 1/50 − 1/2,000 = 39/2,000, so di ≈ 51.3 mm. Autofocus
moves the lens those 1.3 mm.
4.8 Capstone synthesis
One idea behind everything: equals done to equals stay equal.
Capstone synthesis
Step back, and the entire course reduces to a single sentence: algebra replaces
numbers with letters and solves for the unknown by transforming equations without
changing their solutions.
The one principle
a = b ⇒ f(a) = f(b).
Everything you have learned elaborates this statement:
•
Expressions and equations are rewritten and solved by equivalent steps (Week 1).
•
Functions and their graphs turn equations into pictures, and lines into rates (Week
2).
•
Systems, exponents, and polynomials extend the same moves to harder objects
(Week 3).
•
Factoring and the quadratic formula solve every quadratic equation (Week 4).
Where algebra goes next
Algebra 2 extends these ideas to complex numbers, higher-degree polynomials,
logarithms, and conic sections. Geometry, trigonometry, statistics, and calculus all rest
on everything here — calculus, in particular, is algebra applied to limits.

A real example
The quadratic formula has cousins for degree 3 and 4, published by Gerolamo Cardano

algebra, the subject that grew from the question of when x can be isolated.
